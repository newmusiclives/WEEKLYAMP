NEW MUSIC FRIDAY (NOV 6) — KINGS OF LEON, LIL BABY, AND THE HOLIDAY RECORDS

Kings of Leon's O My Beloved lands Friday, November 6. It's their tenth studio album, the follow-up to 2024's Can We Please Have Fun, and their first full-length produced entirely by the band. Thirteen tracks, out through the band's own Love Tap Records with Virgin Music. The press release pitches it as their “most intimate and unguarded record to date”; “My Whole World” was the lead single, with “Cold Blue Dawn” and “KOYSAR” (short for “Kick Off Your Shoes and Run”) since. UK fans: a one-off release show at the 3,800-capacity British Airways ARC at Olympia London follows on Tuesday, November 10.

Lil Baby's Fresh arrives the same day. Seventeen tracks, with Future, Young Thug, and Rylo Rodriguez on the tracklist. It's led by the Pharrell-produced “Dead Fresh,” released back in July, which uDiscover reports peaked at No. 18 on the Billboard Hot 100.

And the holiday season starts early. Alanis Morissette's Good Weather for Immaculate Conception — her first-ever holiday album, out via Epiphany Music/Virgin Music — runs 14 tracks, with two originals (“To an Empath at Christmas Time” and “Christmas on the Dancefloor”) and guests including Brandi Carlile, Steve Martin, and Martin Short. Sufjan Stevens' Worst Christmas Ever! on Asthmatic Kitty is the first time his holiday music has been compiled into a single record: 15 picks from more than a hundred Christmas recordings across the Songs for Christmas and Silver & Gold box sets, plus one new original, “On This Christmas (It Only Gets Better).”

None of these have been heard in full outside the camps yet, so no verdicts here. Reviews in Issue #11.

BACKSTAGE PASS — THE WU-TANG ALBUM THAT EXISTS ONCE

“The Disciple: A Wu-Tang Fable” arrived on Netflix worldwide on October 16, 2026, after premiering at Sundance in January. Directed by Joanna Natasegara, it tells the story of Cilvaringz, the Dutch-Moroccan rapper and producer who went from Wu-Tang superfan to RZA's confidant — and the man behind Once Upon a Time in Shaolin, the 31-track double album of which only one legal copy exists.

If you missed the saga the first time: the album was sold in 2015 and the buyer turned out to be Martin Shkreli, reported to have paid $2 million. After Shkreli's securities-fraud conviction, a federal court seized it in 2018; the Justice Department sold it on in 2021, and PleasrDAO bought it that September for $4 million. Its first official public exhibition came in June 2024 at the Museum of Old and New Art in Tasmania, where listeners signed a no-recording waiver. Under its terms, it can't be commercially released until 2103.

Not every critic is sold. In Review Online called the result “patchy” and “an at times lively but ultimately ever-thickening smokescreen.”

The bigger question it leaves you with: is a one-copy album a radical defence of music's value, or just the ultimate collectible locked away from the fans it was made for? The film doesn't settle it. That's probably why it's worth watching.

STREAMING DASHBOARD — SPOTIFY'S AI REMIX UPSELL

On Friday, October 30, the Financial Times reported that Spotify is planning a paid add-on that would let subscribers use AI to make their own versions of songs — turning a folk track into a dance track, say, or making one artist sound like they're covering another. Per the report, which cites people familiar with the plans, Spotify is considering charging an extra $4–5 a month and aiming for a launch in the first quarter of 2027, possibly unveiling it around the Grammys in February.

Important caveat: this is reporting, not an announcement. Spotify did not immediately respond to Reuters' request for comment, and the price and timing are described as under consideration.

What the FT says is already in place, and what fans should watch:

  • Licensing. Deals are reportedly signed with Universal Music Group, the indie label group Merlin, and Believe. Talks with Sony Music and Warner Music are still going.
  • Royalties and charts. Royalties from fan-made remixes would reportedly go to the original artists, and plays would count toward the original songs on Spotify's charts.
  • The context. The report landed a week after Spotify's Q3 results (October 22): 306 million Premium subscribers, up 9% year on year, 788 million monthly listeners, and €5.0 billion in revenue. The same release announced Fresh Finds Forward, a program Spotify says will support about 10,000 emerging independent artists a year.

The open question for fans is the one the report doesn't answer: how much say individual artists get over AI versions of their songs. If you want more of your money to land with the artists you actually listen to, the most direct lever is still Bandcamp, merch, and tickets bought straight from the artist. Streaming is the discovery layer; the support layer is everywhere else.

CONCERT DISCOVERY — FACE-VALUE RESALE REACHES THE BIG FESTIVALS

If you've ever had to sell a festival ticket you couldn't use, this one's for you.

On October 30, Ticketmaster extended its integration with CashorTrade, the fan-to-fan resale marketplace, to Front Gate Tickets — the Austin-based festival ticketing company owned by Live Nation and Ticketmaster. Front Gate's clients include Lollapalooza, Austin City Limits, Bourbon & Beyond, Lost Lands, and Ultra Music Festival. Tickets are authenticated with Front Gate before resale, every listing is capped at face value or below (checked against the original purchase price, fees included), and the ticket is reissued to the buyer in the CashorTrade app.

“Festival goers should be able to buy resale tickets for festivals knowing they're getting the real thing,” said Andrew Sider, Ticketmaster's EVP of Festivals and General Admission. CashorTrade founder and CEO Brando Rich: “Every new integration is another door we open for fans who just want to see a show and pay the original, face value price.”

The cleaner read: this lands while Ticketmaster is still in court. In April, a federal jury in Manhattan found Live Nation and Ticketmaster liable for illegally maintaining monopoly power and found fans were overcharged $1.72 per ticket; remedies are still to be decided, and Live Nation is expected to appeal. Separately, on October 27 the FTC formally demanded a jury trial in its own case, which alleges Ticketmaster let brokers get around purchase limits and advertised prices without mandatory fees. The companies deny it, and no trial date is set — the FTC and seven states have proposed April 2028.
