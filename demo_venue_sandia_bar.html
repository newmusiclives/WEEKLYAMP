Corrales finally put decibel numbers on paper. Here is what 55 dBA at the neighbour's fence means for a 1948 bar with a rooftop.
A TrueFans NEWSLETTER

Part of the TrueFans Ecosystem™
Issue #1 · Monday, November 2, 2026

Corrales, New Mexico has about 8,500 people, one main road, and an argument about amplified music that has now outlasted two mayors. Last Tuesday, October 27, that argument finally got numbers: the village council discussed a fourth draft of a new noise ordinance with decibel limits measured at the neighbour's property line. It has not been adopted. But for the first time, an operator on Corrales Road can read the rule they will probably be living under next patio season.

This issue is a working file on the Sandia Bar, 4445 Corrales Rd — a 1948 bar that reopened with a new patio and a rooftop, which puts it squarely in the middle of that question. Not a review. If you run a room in a village that likes its quiet, the useful part is the sequence of decisions the operators have made, and what the draft on the council's table now asks of all of them.

Everything below is sourced. Where we could not verify something, we say so rather than filling the gap — the last section is nothing but gaps.

VENUE SPOTLIGHT
A 1948 room, nearly doubled — and what that buys you

The Sandia Bar was established in 1948. The Chavez family ran it continuously until 2018; Steve Chavez was the last family operator, and the bar changed hands following his death. At the 2024 reopening, Lane McIntyre was general manager under the new ownership. (The bar's own site names no manager or owners today — its footer reads only “Sandia Bar LLC” — so we have not been able to reconfirm that.)

The renovation is the part worth studying. Per the Corrales Comment, the work nearly doubled the interior square footage, added a patio and a rooftop area, and converted the family's former private area into public space. The adobe walls stayed, with truth windows cut to expose the original materials, and the vintage Budweiser and Schlitz signs stayed on the wall — the bar's own history page says the signs were refurbished.

Read that as a set of trade-offs rather than a decor story:

  • Doubling the interior raises your fixed nut — staffing, HVAC, insurance — before it sells a single extra pint. In November, when the patio empties, the indoor room has to carry the business on its own.
  • Adding a patio and a rooftop buys the highest-margin square footage in the business. It is also the square footage that makes you audible to your neighbours — and, under the draft ordinance below, the square footage that gets measured.
  • Keeping the adobe and the old signage is not sentiment. In a village that has fought development for decades, visible continuity with 1948 is political capital you will want when you go to the public hearing.

Current published hours (sandiabar.com, checked October 30): bar Sun–Thur 11a–10p, Fri–Sat 11a–12 (the site prints “12p”; we read that as midnight). Restaurant Sun–Thur 11a–9p, Fri–Sat 11a–10p.

HOUSE RULES
Corrales put numbers on paper: 55 dBA at the neighbour's fence

For two years the question in Corrales was whether the noise rule would get a number. As of the October 27 council meeting, the question is which number — and the draft answers it.

Why it is moving now. The current rule bans sound that annoys or disturbs others. Village Attorney Randy Autio warned in 2025 that it “may not be enforceable because of that vagueness.” According to the Corrales Comment, a municipal judge has since declared it unenforceable after an establishment was cited for a noise violation. (The paper does not name the establishment, and neither will we.)

What “Draft Noise Ordinance v4” says. The draft is printed in the October 27 agenda packet. Limits are measured anywhere on the receiving property — your neighbour's land, not your patio:

  • Residential zones A-1, A-2 and H: 55 dBA daytime, 45 dBA nighttime (and 60 / 50 dBC).
  • Zones C, O and M: 65 dBA daytime, 55 dBA nighttime (and 70 / 60 dBC).
  • “Daytime” is 7 a.m.–10 p.m. Friday and Saturday, and 7 a.m.–9 p.m. Sunday through Thursday.
  • Where a source is measured across more than one zone, the stricter zone's limit applies at the boundary.
  • Live music is measured over at least five minutes with a Type 2 (or better) meter, and the officer has to attribute the sound to a specific property.
  • If background noise is already within 5 dB of the limit, the test switches to background plus 5 dB.
  • At night, amplified sound that is plainly audible beyond your property line can be a violation without any meter reading at all.

Two things operators asked for in earlier rounds are not in v4. There is no commercial exemption for Corrales Road — the reported 2024 idea of exempting businesses along a stretch of the road is not in this draft; the only event exemption is for “officially sanctioned Village events.” And while the Comment reports that the revisions include “a dedicated permitting process for amplified outdoor sound,” we could not find a permit section in the v4 text in the packet. It may be coming in v5. Ask before you count on it.

Where the debate stands. At the meeting, Councilor Zachary Burkett called the draft well written and said it should move forward, while expecting changes. Resident Sabine Shurter, who reported her own decibel readings during the 2025 debate, argued that low-frequency bass carries far. Resident Sybil Keyser countered that it is really a music ordinance, since it leaves out sources like yard equipment and traffic. (The draft does in fact exempt daytime lawn equipment, construction and farm work.)

What happens next: the attorney and administration redraft. The new version goes back to the council for publication. Then comes a formal public hearing, then a final vote — the Comment says the aim is new rules “by the time the springtime patio music season begins.” An ordinance would most likely take effect 30 days after passage. No hearing date has been set as of October 30.

What an operator should do with that: the cheap move — measure your own property line before anyone else does — is now a precise one. Get a Type 2 meter. With a neighbour's permission, stand on the nearest residential lot and take a five-minute average during your loudest set. Then do the same with the music off. If you are anywhere near 55 dBA — or 60 dBC, which is the line that catches bass — you know before the public hearing whether to argue about the number or fix the room.

THE DOOR
A free 2–5pm weekend slot is a bar-spend bet — and now a compliance bet too

Sandia Bar's published live music is Saturdays and Sundays, 2–5pm, free, on the patio, listed as a family-friendly daytime slot with a full bar. The Visit Albuquerque listing was still up when we checked on October 30, with no end date.

Four things follow from that structure, and they are worth stating plainly because a lot of operators pick this format by accident rather than on purpose.

1. There is no door revenue, so the music is a cost line, not a revenue line. It has to be justified entirely by extra bar and kitchen spend against the same hours without music. That is a measurable thing — same weekday, music weeks against non-music weeks — and if you are not measuring it you are guessing.

2. Free-and-daytime buys you the widest possible audience and the lowest possible spend-per-head. Families come. Families order one round and a basket of something. Your revenue per head at 3pm will not look like your revenue per head at 10pm, and it is a mistake to hold the afternoon slot to the evening's numbers.

3. Under the draft, it is the safest slot on the calendar. 2–5pm sits well inside the draft's “daytime” window on both weekend days, so it faces the higher daytime limits. It also never touches the nighttime “plainly audible” clause, the one that needs no meter. An evening patio set that runs past 9pm on a Sunday would. If you are choosing which music to protect, this is the one.

4. November is the real test. A patio slot depends on the weather. We could not confirm whether the weekend music runs through the winter or moves indoors. Neither the listing nor the bar's site says. Ask before you plan a December around it.

We have not published a revenue model for this room because Sandia Bar's capacity, covers, and spend-per-head are not public and we will not invent them. If you run a comparable room, the arithmetic above is the frame; your own POS data is the only honest input.

BOOKING DESK
The booking channel is a web form — here's what to ask before you fill it in

Sandia Bar takes artist pitches through a form on its own site — the nav item reads “Play Music at Sandia Bar?” and points at sandiabar.com/live-music-submission/. There is no named talent buyer published, and no public booking email.

That is a genuinely common setup and it is not a red flag. A form filters volume for a room without a full-time buyer. But it does mean the terms are not published, and the terms are the whole negotiation. The site does not state pay structure, set length, whether a PA is provided, backline, load-in time, or how far ahead the calendar is built.

So the pitch email — or the form's notes field — should be the one that makes those questions easy to answer. For a free daytime patio slot, the five that matter:

  1. How is the artist paid? Flat fee, percentage of bar over a baseline, or tips only. All three are legitimate for a no-cover afternoon; only one of them is a surprise after the fact.
  2. Is there a house PA, and who runs it? An outdoor patio slot with no house system means you are carrying everything, and it changes what the fee has to cover.
  3. Set length and format. Three hours is a long afternoon. One act for 2–5pm is a very different ask from two 75-minute sets.
  4. Does the slot run through the winter, and where? The 2024 reporting said live music was planned “likely on a seasonal basis, depending on local regulations.” It is November, and the regulations are still being written.
  5. Is there a house volume limit? New this issue. With a draft ordinance built on measured decibels, a room that hands artists a number now is protecting them as well as itself. A room that doesn't may hand them one next spring, mid-set.

For operators reading this from the other side of the form: publishing even three of those five on the submission page would cut your unusable-pitch volume sharply. Artists self-select out when the terms are visible. The rooms that publish terms get fewer pitches and better ones.

BAR & BOARD
The hours moved again — and the calendar got some non-music nights

A small timeline that tells you something about sequencing.

In September 2024, at reopening, the kitchen was still under construction, targeting the end of that year. In the meantime the bar ran on food trucks — Wanna Taco Bout Burgers and BigBoss HotLinks, on a rotating schedule. The 2024 reporting listed early-week hours of Monday–Tuesday 2–10 p.m.

By August 2026 the site listed separate restaurant hours and an 11am open every day, with the bar running to 11pm Wednesday and Thursday. As of October 30, the bar's Sunday–Thursday hours are one block, 11a–10p, so Wednesday and Thursday close an hour earlier than they did in August. Restaurant hours have not changed. We don't know why the bar hours changed, and we won't guess.

The other change is on the events page. Alongside a live-music tile, it now shows trivia and game day tiles (no dates are posted for any of them). That is the normal winter move for a patio room: you program things that work indoors, at conversation volume, when the patio is cold.

The food-truck bridge is still the transferable idea. It let the room open, build a habit, and trade for a year without waiting on a hood, a grease trap, and an inspection. The risk is that you train your customers to expect the truck; the fix is to take the food revenue in-house as soon as the kitchen is legal, which appears to be what happened here.

THE NEIGHBOURHOOD
Your parking overflow and your volume are the same conversation

Sandia Bar's own site still notes that its parking lot “has been full a lot” and directs overflow to Village of Corrales public parking areas.

The village has been working the parking side too. In June, the council reached a compromise on adding public parking on village-owned land. The two proposals on the table involved the Anderson and Gonzales properties, though the Comment's account does not spell out the final split. It came after an earlier idea of “no parking” signs on residential streets that, Councilor Burkett said, many residents opposed.

Put that next to the noise file and you have the complete picture of what a village actually experiences when a room gets busy. In the 2025 debate, Corrales resident Sabine Shurter reported measuring 65 decibels at her home for several hours some nights, sometimes as high as 76. We don't know what meter, weighting or averaging she used, so don't hold those readings against the draft's numbers as if they were the same measurement. But even read loosely, they sit well above the draft's 45 dBA night limit for residential land.

Parking overflow and amplified sound are the same complaint wearing two hats. Both are your success becoming your neighbour's Saturday. And both get raised at the same council meeting, usually by the same people.

The practical read: a room that has already routed its overflow to designated public parking has done the visible, documentable thing — it has an answer when asked. The equivalent move on sound is a written volume policy and a property-line measurement you took yourself. During the 2025 debate, then-Mayor Jim Fahey said of the venues: “We want them to be able to survive.” The public hearing on the next draft is where that sentiment either turns into a workable number or doesn't. Operators who show up with readings get heard on the number. Operators who show up after the first citation are arguing about a rule that has already passed.

WHAT WE COULD NOT VERIFY
The gaps, stated rather than filled

A venue newsletter that invents a capacity is worse than useless. Here is everything we wanted for this issue and could not confirm from a source we could read:

  • Capacity — indoor, patio, or rooftop. Not published anywhere we found.
  • PA, backline, and who engineers the patio slot. Not stated.
  • Artist pay structure for the weekend afternoon slot. Not stated.
  • Whether the weekend music runs through November and winter, and whether it moves indoors. The listing is a recurring weekly slot with no end date; the 2024 reporting said “likely on a seasonal basis.” We did not confirm which applies this month.
  • Current and upcoming performers. The events page shows tiles with no dates or names. The venue's Facebook and Instagram are the live calendar, and neither was machine-readable for us, so we will not summarise a lineup we have not seen.
  • Who runs the bar today. The 2024 reporting names Lane McIntyre as general manager; nothing current confirms or contradicts it. The ownership group has never been named in anything we could read.
  • Which zone Sandia Bar's nearest neighbours sit in — that decides whether the 55 or the 65 dBA daytime line applies at their fence. We did not find a zoning map we could read for 4445 Corrales Rd.
  • The permit process. The Comment describes one; the v4 draft text we read has none. We will report v5 when it is published.
  • The public hearing date. Not set as of October 30.

Roz Whitaker, Editor

Every one of these villages has the same fight, and it is never really about decibels. It is about whether the place you love staying the place you love requires it to stop changing. A 1948 bar with a new rooftop is the whole argument in one building. Now there is a number on the table — 55 at the fence — and a hearing coming. I don't think there's a clean answer, but I notice the rooms that survive it are the ones that showed up to the meeting with a reading instead of a grievance. — PS
