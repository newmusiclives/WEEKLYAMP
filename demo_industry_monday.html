Issue #10 · November 2, 2026
From the Editor

Welcome to Issue #10. Q3 earnings season landed over the past fortnight, and the two biggest reports in the business arrived exactly a week apart. Spotify reported 306 million paying subscribers and €672 million in quarterly operating income. Universal Music grew revenue 12.3% — and then Sir Lucian Grainge spent the call arguing that the way streaming services pay labels “has to change”. Underneath the headline numbers, the AI fight moved into discovery: Suno is now trying to force Apple to testify about its licensing talks with Universal and Sony. Meanwhile, MBW revealed that Create Music Group quietly bought into DistroKid — the same distributor Universal sued in September. Platform, label, distributor, AI startup: every layer of the stack is negotiating with every other layer at once.

Every claim in this issue carries a source link. If a fact isn't sourced, it isn't here.

— Jordan Voss, Editor, Industry Edition

INDUSTRY PULSE

Universal Music's Q3: 12.3% growth, but only 5.3% of it is organic.

Universal Music Group reported third-quarter results on Thursday, October 29. Revenue for the three months to September 30 was €3,394 million, up 12.3% year-over-year (11.7% in constant currency). Adjusted EBITDA came in at €729 million, up 9.0% in constant currency, for an adjusted margin of 21.5%.

The number to watch is the one in brackets. UMG has consolidated Downtown since February 20, 2026, and stripping it out, constant-currency revenue growth was 5.3%. The same pattern runs through the segments:

  • Subscription streaming: up 19.2% in constant currency; 9.5% excluding Downtown. UMG credits part of that to pricing benefits from its “Streaming 2.0” agreements.
  • Ad-supported streaming: up 13.5% in constant currency; 4.7% excluding Downtown.
  • Physical: up 17.4% in constant currency.
  • Music Publishing: revenue of €590 million, up 7.5% in constant currency but just 1.5% excluding Downtown, with publishing adjusted EBITDA down 3.5% to €136 million. CFO Matt Ellis acknowledged that publishing growth “was below the level we expect from this business over time.”

Why it matters. Downtown is doing a lot of the heavy lifting in UMG's headline growth — and Downtown's core business is serving independent artists and labels. Grainge also announced “the next phase of our transformation, which will run through to the end of 2028,” covering how UMG negotiates and prices platform agreements. More detail is promised with full-year results. (More on what he wants from DSPs in Rights & Royalties, below.)

DEAL RADAR

Create Music Group owns a piece of DistroKid.

Music Business Worldwide reported on Wednesday, October 28, that Create Music Group holds a minority stake in DistroKid, acquired earlier this year. The size of the stake and the price have not been disclosed. It sits alongside CVC Capital Partners' majority investment in DistroKid, announced in July and closed in Q3; existing backer Insight Partners keeps a “significant minority stake”, and Phil Bauer remains president. No valuation has been reported for the CVC deal — MBW reported in January that $2 billion was being discussed in talks with potential buyers, but that was never confirmed.

The timing is awkward. Universal Music sued DistroKid in Delaware federal court on September 15, alleging deceptive trade practices and copyright infringement, and accusing the distributor of flooding platforms with AI-generated content that diverts revenue from legitimate rightsholders. DistroKid's deadline to respond is December 4.

Also on the wire last week:

  • Peermusic buys 120 Publishing (Oct 28). Peermusic acquired the Sydney-based publisher, which Tim McGee founded in 2009 for Ministry of Sound Australia. Peermusic says the deal covers about 5,000 works and 120,000 administered copyrights, with a focus on dance and electronic writers and producers. Terms were not disclosed. 120 Publishing GM Karen Hamilton becomes MD of Peermusic Australia.
  • Mary J. Blige partners with Primary Wave (Oct 26). The deal covers her composition and recording catalogues, including “Family Affair” and “Be Without You.” It is announced as a “partnership”; the announcement does not say whether Primary Wave took an ownership stake, and terms were not disclosed.

What it signals. Distribution is now a strategic asset in its own right, not plumbing. A private-equity majority owner, an independent music company as a minority holder, and the biggest of the three majors as a litigant — all circling the same DIY distributor in a single year.

AI & MUSIC LAB

Suno wants Apple on the stand.

The Universal/Sony lawsuit against Suno has pulled in a company that isn't a party to it — and has a lot to lose from discovery. Apple filed a motion on October 13 to quash a deposition subpoena from Suno. Last Tuesday, October 27, Suno filed a 24-page opposition. It wants Apple to testify on four topics:

  • Apple's current licensing negotiations with Universal and Sony;
  • whether Apple has assessed running Apple Music without one or both of those catalogues;
  • streaming fraud on the service;
  • Apple's communications with the labels or the RIAA about Suno and the case.

Suno's argument, per its filing, is that the majors “use their copyright monopoly and ‘must have’ catalog as a cudgel to extract broad, anti-AI concessions from DSPs.” Apple calls the topics “irrelevant, overbroad, and disproportionate to the needs of the case” and “a fishing expedition by a new entrant to the music industry,” and says it has already responded to six subpoenas. A hearing is set for December 1 before Magistrate Judge Virginia K. DeMarchi in California; the underlying case remains in Massachusetts federal court.

The bigger picture. This is the second front. On September 18, Universal, Sony and their affiliated labels filed a separate suit against Suno covering 60,202 recordings not included in the original 2024 case, after the court declined to let them add the works to it. The new complaint also alleges Suno “stream ripped” audio from YouTube. Warner isn't a plaintiff — it settled with Suno and signed a licensing deal in November 2025.

Why it matters. If Suno gets its deposition, the confidential terms on which the biggest DSPs license the biggest catalogues — including any AI restrictions — edge toward the record. Every distributor and indie label negotiating its own DSP or AI deal has an interest in how December 1 goes.

MONEY MOVES

Spotify Q3: 306 million subscribers, €672 million operating income.

Spotify reported its third quarter on Thursday, October 22, and its shareholder update says all of its KPIs met or exceeded guidance. The headline figures:

  • Premium subscribers: 306 million, up 9% year-over-year.
  • Monthly active users: 788 million, up 11%.
  • Revenue: €4,969 million, up 16% (14% in constant currency). Premium revenue was €4,487 million (+17%); ad-supported revenue was €482 million (+10%).
  • Premium ARPU: €4.95, up 9.3% (7.5% in constant currency). Spotify attributes the currency-neutral growth to price increases, partly offset by product and market mix.
  • Profitability: gross margin of 33.0%, operating income of €672 million, free cash flow of €735 million.

For Q4, Spotify is guiding to 316 million subscribers, 818 million MAUs, €5.2 billion in revenue, a 33.8% gross margin and €820 million in operating income.

Read it alongside UMG. Spotify says its premium gross margin improved because revenue growth outpaced music costs. A week later, UMG credited part of its own subscription growth to “pricing benefits from Streaming 2.0 agreements” and wholesale price increases. Both sides of the table are doing well. That is exactly the backdrop against which a label decides to reopen the deal structure.

RIGHTS & ROYALTIES

Grainge: “The structure of our DSP agreements has to change.”

Presenting UMG's Q3 results last Thursday, Sir Lucian Grainge put pro-rata payment squarely in the frame. He criticised streaming services for “treating AI slop as the same as the greatest songs in the world,” and said “the uniform application of pro-rata payments makes the problem worse.” His position: unethically generated AI tracks should not earn the same as music with lasting demand.

Grainge framed the goal as “win-win partnerships with DSPs that properly reward their work and ours,” with compensation that better matches the value of different content. He did not name which DSP agreements are up for renewal or when. On AI, he said UMG is “moving from principles to products, and from products to revenue,” and expects AI partnerships to “begin generating incremental revenue in fiscal ’27.”

For indie artists and labels: paying some streams more than others is a rights question, not just a commercial one. Under pro-rata, a stream is a stream. The next round of label–DSP contracts may decide that some streams are worth more than others — and the independent sector, which doesn't sit at that table, will inherit whatever definition the majors negotiate. UMG's lawsuit against DistroKid (see Deal Radar) shows the same argument being made in court.
