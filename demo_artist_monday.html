VOCAL BOOTH — THE STRAW EXERCISE, FROM THE ACTUAL SCIENCE

If you've taken a voice lesson in the last decade, you've probably been told to phonate into a straw. The exercise gets passed around like folklore in the singing community, but the underlying research is real and worth understanding once instead of taking on faith.

The technique is one of a family of semi-occluded vocal tract exercises (SOVTEs), and the best-known explainer of the physics is Ingo Titze, whose vocology work is based at the University of Utah. Writing in the Journal of Singing in 2018, Titze put it plainly: the exercises' efficacy “is no longer challenged” — what's inconsistent is practitioners' explanations of why they work. Utah's vocology site also hosts his introduction to straw phonation.

What's actually happening when you phonate through a straw, in plain terms:

  • The narrow exit builds steady pressure in the vocal tract above the vocal folds — back pressure — which spreads the upper portion of the folds slightly apart.
  • With the folds parallel and slightly separated, the pressure needed to start phonation drops, and the vocal tract's acoustic inertance lowers it further.
  • The result, per Titze: vibrational amplitude and collision forces are reduced, so you can take lung pressure and pitch high in a glide without the folds slamming together.

Translation: you can warm up vigorously, including to high pitch and with plenty of support, while the system keeps contact stress on the folds low — Titze describes it as self-regulating. One practical note from the same paper: the straw's diameter matters most. Narrower means more resistance; plastic versus glass barely matters.

The practical upshot for working artists: the straw warm-up isn't just a folk remedy. It's a measurably safer way to get to the top of your range before a show or a session, and the research is publicly available if you want to read past the YouTube tutorials. Links below.

SONGCRAFT — HOOKTHEORY'S MOST-USED PROGRESSION

If you wanted a single, defensible answer to “what's the most common chord progression in popular music?” the closest thing to one comes out of Hooktheory's TheoryTab database, which now lists more than 80,000 song analyses. Their public Trends tool lets you interrogate which chords most often follow which, based on user-contributed song transcriptions.

The headline finding: Hooktheory's own progressions page labels I – V – vi – IV “the most popular progression” — in the key of C, that's C – G – Am – F. It's the same four chords behind the “four chord song” medley joke, and the joke works because the progression really is a centre of gravity for Western pop.

What's useful for songwriters is not the single progression but the tool itself: you can build any progression in Hooktheory's interface and get back a list of every song in their database that uses it, plus the empirically most-likely next chord based on the corpus. It is the closest thing the field has to a public, queryable map of pop harmony.

Two practical uses:

  • If you've written a verse-chorus that feels generic, paste the progression into the Trends tool. If your sequence is high-frequency, deliberately push one chord one position off the most-likely path — that single substitution is often the difference between “heard this before” and “hooks me.”
  • If you're stuck on a bridge, query the chord that ends your chorus and let the most-probable next-chord list seed your bridge starting point. You're not copying anything — you're using empirical data to break out of writer's lock.

GEAR GARAGE — THE TWO MICS STILL WORTH YOUR MONEY

Gear coverage is mostly noise. But two studio mics, both launched in 2023, keep coming up when working artists ask what to buy for a project room — and three years on, they're still the right answers to two different problems.

RØDE NT1 5th Generation — the quiet-room condenser

RØDE's product page lists self-noise of 4 dBA — “astonishingly low”, in its own words. Two features make this mic interesting beyond the noise spec: it offers both XLR and USB output through RØDE's Dual Connect design, and over USB it records in 32-bit float, which means clipping is functionally a non-issue — you adjust level in post and the take survives.

For a singer-songwriter who tracks vocals at home and brings stems to a mixer, that combination is the point: quiet performances captured without an audible noise floor, and no unrecoverable clipped take when the chorus comes in louder than the soundcheck.

Shure SM7dB — launched at $499

The SM7B has been the broadcast and vocal-rock workhorse for decades, but it needs a lot of clean gain — either an interface with plenty to spare, or an in-line booster like a Cloudlifter. Shure's SM7dB, announced September 27, 2023, builds the booster in: a phantom-powered preamp adding +18 dB or +28 dB, with a bypass when you don't need it. Shure says the preamp uses technology licensed from Cloud, the company behind the Cloudlifter.

If you're running an SM7B through an external booster, the SM7dB folds that box into the mic. If your room is quiet and you want detail, the NT1 5th Gen is the value play. Check current street prices before you buy — they move.

STREAMING DASHBOARD — SPOTIFY'S AI YEAR, AND THE NEXT ROYALTY FIGHT

Spotify put out a run of artist-relevant news in the last two weeks: an October 20 one-year update on its AI and anti-spam rules, a licensing deal the same day, and its October 22 third-quarter results. Here's what matters for working musicians:

1. Artist Profile Protection is coming to everyone. The tool lets you review releases that list you as an artist before they appear on your profile — the fix for impostor uploads and mis-credited tracks. Thousands of artists joined the beta; Spotify says it will be available to all artists by the end of the year. Watch your Spotify for Artists account for it.

2. Verification and AI labels are now real filters. Spotify says more than 300,000 artists are now verified, and more than 70% of them are independent. Profiles it believes are AI-generated get an AI Persona badge and, by default, are kept out of editorial and algorithmic recommendations unless a listener follows them. AI Credits, launched in April, let you say which roles and instruments involved AI — Spotify says artists are submitting tens of thousands a day.

3. The spam purge is big. Spotify says it has taken more than 50 million slop and spam tracks out of recommendations this year and removed more than 75 million spammy tracks from the platform entirely. The 1,000-stream royalty threshold, in place since 2024, stays — and Spotify says tracks flagged as likely AI-generated are 10 times more likely to fall below it.

4. A new program for emerging indies. In its Q3 results (306 million Premium subscribers, 788 million monthly users), Spotify announced Fresh Finds Forward, aimed at helping roughly 10,000 emerging independent artists a year. Spotify hasn't said how to get in yet — we'll report it when it does.

Also on October 20: Believe and its TuneCore platform signed a deal with Spotify covering an upcoming tool for AI-powered fan remixes and covers, planned as a paid add-on for Premium. It's opt-in: TuneCore artists first opt in to being considered for AI programs, then decide on this one. No launch date yet.

Meanwhile, in Washington. Proposals were filed October 5 in Phonorecords V, the Copyright Royalty Board case that sets U.S. streaming mechanical rates for songwriters for 2028–2032. A coalition backed by the Songwriters Guild of America and others is asking for a flat $0.003 per stream. NMPA and NSAI want the greater of several measures, including 20% of service revenue or $0.004 per play. Spotify, Apple and YouTube want to keep the current 15.35% headline rate; Amazon wants it cut to 13.4%. Nothing is decided: hearings are scheduled for March 2027 and the judges' initial decision is due December 17, 2027.
